The multimeter: try one, then learn every setting

A multimeter measures voltage, current and resistance in one case. It tells you what is happening inside a circuit without taking it apart, if you connect it the right way.

Before you touch anythingThis guide teaches ideas and does not replace a qualified electrician or your local electrical code. Read the safety limits.

The lab: attach the probes, read the circuit, find the fault

Turn the dial, choose the red-lead socket, drag the red and black probe tips onto two numbered points (or use the lists), and read the display. Some settings are wrong on purpose: they teach by failing. In Free play the circuit shows its state. In Case files it hides it, and you diagnose a fault from your readings alone. Nothing here can hurt you; a real meter on a real battery bank can.

What to expect from a battery

A resting battery's voltage hints at its charge, but only after several hours with no load or charging, at room temperature, and only for the right chemistry. For a lead-acid battery the table below is a rough guide:

LiFePO4 is differentA 12 V (12.8 V nominal) LiFePO4 battery rests at about 13.3 to 13.6 V when full, and its voltage stays nearly flat between roughly 20% and 90% charge. You cannot read its state of charge from a multimeter; use a battery monitor that counts amp-hours. Reading 12.6 V on one does not mean "full".

What to expect from a solar panel

An unloaded panel reads its open-circuit voltage (Voc). A panel made for 12 V systems typically reads about 21 to 23 V in sun with nothing connected, and about 18 V (its Vmp) while delivering full power. A larger 400 W panel has a Voc around 44 V. Panels in series add: two panels with a Voc of 44.6 V read 89.2 V. Voltage rises further in the cold; see the Solar page. Use a meter and leads rated for the full string voltage, and see the CAT note below.

Common relationships: 1 W = 1 V x 1 A. 1,000 W = 1 kW. 1,000 mA = 1 A. 1 kWh = 1,000 Wh. A 60 Hz supply is typical of North America and a 50 Hz supply of much of the world; see the AC page for the list.

CAT ratings

A meter's CAT rating (CAT II, III or IV) with a voltage figure (for example "CAT III 600 V") tells you how much transient energy it is built to withstand on a given part of a building's wiring. Roughly, CAT II covers sockets and appliances, CAT III covers distribution boards and fixed installations, CAT IV covers the supply entrance. Use a meter and leads rated for the system you are measuring. A rating for AC wiring is not a promise for a high-voltage DC solar string: confirm the meter is rated for that DC voltage, and if it is not, do not measure it.

Safety rules

  • Resistance and continuity only with power off.
  • Never put the meter in current mode across a battery or panel. The short circuit blows the fuse or the meter.
  • Check the red lead's socket before every measurement, and put it back to the voltage socket after current.
  • Inspect the leads. Do not use cracked or damaged insulation.
  • Prove your meter on a known live source before trusting a "dead" reading, and again after.
  • Know the rating of the meter and the system, and stop if you are not sure.
