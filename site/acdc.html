Step 2 of 5 · AC vs DC

Direct and alternating current

Batteries and solar panels make DC. Houses and most appliances use AC. An inverter sits between them, and it is not free.

Before you touch anythingThis guide teaches ideas and does not replace a qualified electrician or your local electrical code. Read the safety limits.

The difference

DC: one direction

The current flows one way at a steady voltage. Solar panels, batteries, USB, cars and RVs are DC.

AC: back and forth

The voltage swings in a sine wave and the current reverses direction 50 or 60 times a second. The grid, generators and wall sockets are AC. Transformers can step AC voltage up and down, which is why the grid uses it.

The "120 V" or "230 V" on an AC supply is the effective (RMS) value, the voltage that heats a resistor like a DC supply of the same number. The wave itself peaks higher: 120 V RMS peaks at about 170 V, and 230 V RMS at about 325 V (RMS x 1.414).

Mains supply by region

Nominal values; the allowed tolerance is wide. Check the label on any equipment you plan to run.

The inverter in the middle

An inverter changes battery DC into AC. Two numbers matter. Its efficiency is the share of battery energy that arrives as AC power; this site assumes 90%, a typical figure, so a 100 W AC load costs the battery about 111 W. Its standby draw is what it uses just by being on; this site assumes 10 W, which is 240 Wh a day.

Where you can, run lights, pumps and chargers directly on DC and keep the inverter for what needs AC. The Simulator shows what that is worth: switch loads between AC and DC items and watch the daily total.

Pure sine or modified sine

A pure sine wave inverter makes a clean copy of mains power and suits electronics, motors and anything with a power supply. Cheaper modified-sine inverters make a stepped wave that some equipment tolerates badly (buzzing, extra heat, reduced life). If in doubt, choose pure sine.

Watts, volt-amps and motors

For a heater or a lamp, watts are volts times amps. For motors and many electronics on AC, the current and voltage are out of step, so the supply must carry more volt-amps than the appliance uses in watts (the ratio is the power factor). Motors can also draw several times their running current for a moment when starting, so an inverter's surge rating matters. Both are reasons to read the label of the real appliance.
